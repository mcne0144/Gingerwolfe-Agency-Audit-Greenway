## 0. How this document is used

This is a **draft policy skeleton for salespeople who post about the store from their personal accounts** (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, or any other). The operator (Shannon McNeil) adapts it per store and introduces it **one-on-one with each GM** during Month 2. It is not a group rollout, not a policy email, and not a surveillance program.

Why one-on-one: salesperson posting on personal accounts is a **real and sensitive issue at these stores** (staff members already drive a large share of positive reviews and online visibility). A group announcement about it would read as suspicion of the whole team. A one-on-one conversation frames it as what it is: an **opportunity to use the team's visibility safely, with risk managed**, and it lets each GM shape the version that fits their store.

Framing rule (from `OPERATING_RULES.md` §10): every conversation starts from **opportunity and risk management**, never from a list of what people did wrong. If a specific post has already caused a problem, that goes to the GM one-on-one as a coaching conversation, not a compliance lecture.

Nothing here invents legal requirements. Platform-specific rules, FTC guidance, and OEM rules are marked **TO VERIFY — request from client via Shannon McNeil** until Shannon confirms them. No agent contacts any salesperson, manager, or GM.

## 3. What is encouraged (the opportunity)

Salespeople posting from personal accounts, when done well, is one of the cheapest reach a dealership has. The framework encourages:

1. **Authentic behind-the-scenes.** A salesperson's own photos and video of delivery day, the detail bay, a clean showroom, a morning walk of the lot. First-person, casual, real. This is content the store's own pages cannot fake.
2. **Real inventory shares, with compliance.** Sharing a specific vehicle that is verifiably on the lot, with correct model/trim/year and a price path confirmed by the store. The salesperson tags the store's official page and links to the store's listing or site. Details must come from the store's real listing, never from memory (see §6 sourcing).
3. **Service and customer wins.** A finished service job, a customer pickup photo (with the customer's consent), a "we got it done" post. Proof that the store delivers.
4. **Store milestones and local moments.** New model arrival, community event the store actually runs, staff wins like certifications or tenure anniversaries. Real events only.
5. **Review asks done right.** Pointing a happy customer to the store's Google Business Profile review link after a delivery or service visit is encouraged. Asking for reviews in exchange for anything of value is prohibited (see §4).

What makes encouraged content work: it is the salesperson's own voice, it shows a real place, and it points back to the store's official pages rather than replacing them.

## 4. What is prohibited (the risk list)

These apply to any post that mentions the store, its vehicles, its prices, or its staff:

1. **Misleading claims.** No invented prices, payments, APR, mileage, condition, features, availability, or "only N left" unless the limit is real and confirmed. No photo of a vehicle passed off as available when it is not on the lot.
2. **OEM violations.** No claims about manufacturer programs, warranties, certifications, or incentives unless the store has confirmed the exact wording is allowed. OEM rules for Kia and Ford differ, and each store's rules are **TO VERIFY — request from client via Shannon McNeil** — never guessed (see `GOVERNANCE_MODEL.md` §5.1).
3. **Competitor attacks.** No posts about another dealership: no price comparisons against named dealers, no "we beat X," no negative comments about a competitor's stock or service.
4. **Pricing errors.** No price or payment figures typed from memory. A wrong price on a personal account is still the store's price in public. Any figure comes from the store's current listing or the sales manager.
5. **Unapproved incentives.** No personal offers, "mention my name" discounts, or bonus promises beyond what the store has approved. Only the store publishes offers.
6. **Fake reviews and fake engagement.** No buying reviews, no review rings, no asking friends to post reviews, no incentivized reviews (Google prohibits them — `audit/BENCHMARKS.md` §3.2). No astroturfing: a salesperson never posts as a customer.
7. **Confidential or private information.** No customer info, deal terms, trade-in values, or internal store numbers.
8. **Controversy and brand risk.** No political, religious, or divisive posts that connect the store's name to the salesperson's personal opinions; no posting as the store's voice at all. Personal accounts stay personal; the store's pages are the store's voice (see §6).
9. **Impersonation.** No account or post that looks like the dealership itself. A salesperson is a person, not the brand. Handles, bios, and profile photos must not imitate the store's official pages.

---

## 5. Required disclosures

1. **When the salesperson is selling, say so.** A post that shows a specific vehicle and invites a purchase is advertising, and platform and regulator rules may require it to be labeled. The standard practice is a clear "I'm a salesperson at [store]" or platform-native ad labeling. **What each platform requires is TO VERIFY — request from client via Shannon McNeil** — the operator confirms current platform guidance (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn) before this line is finalized for any store.
2. **#ad where required.** The draft policy includes a default rule: any post that promotes a specific vehicle, offer, or the store itself uses a visible disclosure (e.g., "#ad" or "I work at [store]") in the post and, for video, spoken on camera. **The exact requirement per platform and per jurisdiction is TO VERIFY — request from client via Shannon McNeil.** The team does not invent legal claims about what platforms or regulators require.
3. **Tagging done right.** Posts that promote inventory tag the store's official page and link to the store's real listing. A personal post that merely mentions the store needs no disclosure beyond honesty about who the poster is.
4. **When in doubt, disclose.** The default rule for the pilot: a salesperson who would not be comfortable saying the post out loud at a team meeting should not post it. Disclosure questions go to the sales manager, who routes via Shannon if the answer is not already in the store's adopted version.

## 6. Personal-brand vs dealership-brand boundaries

1. **Own the account, own the risk.** The salesperson's personal account is theirs. The store does not require access to it, does not post from it, and does not monitor it (see §7). But a post that mentions the store makes the store visible, so the store's rules apply to that post.
2. **The store's pages are the store's voice.** Official pages (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, GBP) are governed by `GOVERNANCE_MODEL.md` — one approval chain, Shannon publishes. A salesperson never posts as the store, never comments as the store, and never answers customer complaints as the store from a personal account.
3. **Handle and bio hygiene.** The personal account keeps a personal identity. If a salesperson wants to signal their job, the recommended line is a factual bio ("[Name] — sales at [Store name]") that is clearly a person, not the dealership brand. Anything that mimics the store's official page is prohibited (§4.9).
4. **Customer conversations stay on official channels.** When a customer messages a salesperson on a personal account about a deal, the salesperson may respond personally but moves the substantive conversation to the store's official channels (phone, store page, store DMs) at the first reasonable point. This keeps the store's records, response-time expectations, and CRM intact, and it means the pilot's message metrics (`reporting/GM_KPI_FRAMEWORK.md` metric 3) capture the conversation.
5. **The store's content calendar wins.** A salesperson's post never contradicts, preempts, or undercuts what the store's pages publish. If a salesperson wants to share a store post, they share the store's actual post rather than paraphrasing it.

---

## 7. Monitoring and reporting without surveillance

The pilot does not monitor salespeople. The framework relies on a visible policy, a clear reporting path, and coaching — not on watching feeds.

1. **No surveillance.** The store does not pull, scrape, or require access to personal accounts. There is no monitoring tool in this framework, and the team does not build one. Nothing here authorizes watching employees' personal feeds.
2. **Response to complaints, not to feeds.** The trigger for action is a **reported problem**, not an observed post. If a customer, another store, the OEM, or a staff member raises a post, that becomes a one-on-one coaching conversation with the salesperson via the GM and sales manager.
3. **One reporting path.** Complaints about a salesperson's post go to the sales manager or GM. The GM brings it to Shannon if the post may breach OEM or platform rules, so the draft rules get confirmed rather than improvised.
4. **Coaching before consequence.** The first response to a reported post is a private conversation: what the rule is, why it exists, what the post did wrong. Only repeat or deliberate breaches escalate to the GM's normal personnel process. The framework is a risk-management tool, not a disciplinary instrument.
5. **What the store can do passively.** The store may, at its discretion, ask that a specific post be taken down if it breaches the adopted rules. That is a request to the employee, not a right to their account.
6. **Patterns, not persons.** If multiple reports cluster on one topic (pricing errors, OEM claims), the GM updates the store's version of this framework or runs a short team huddle on that topic. Patterns get fixed; individuals get coached privately.
7. **Good faith is assumed.** The framework assumes salespeople want to help the store. Its tone in every conversation is opportunity plus risk management, and it says so out loud.

## 8. Store-team training checklist (per store, after the GM adopts a version)

The GM runs this with the sales manager, via Shannon's support. One session, 20–30 minutes, at a team meeting the store already holds — not a special "content compliance" meeting that reads like suspicion.

1. ☐ GM introduces the framework as opportunity: "Our team's posts reach people our store pages don't. Here is how we keep that a good thing."
2. ☐ Walk the encouraged list (§3) with real examples from this store (examples TO VERIFY — request from client via Shannon McNeil; if none yet, use generic shapes, never invented store facts).
3. ☐ Walk the prohibited list (§4) plainly. Emphasize the two that hurt stores most: wrong prices and OEM claims.
4. ☐ State the disclosure rule the store adopted (§5) and where to ask questions (sales manager → Shannon for platform-rule questions).
5. ☐ State the boundaries (§6): personal accounts stay personal; the store's pages are the store's voice; move customer deals to official channels.
6. ☐ State the reporting path (§7): a customer or manager raises an issue; the first response is a private coaching conversation.
7. ☐ Confirm the vehicle-detail rule: before posting a vehicle, check the store's current listing; never type price from memory.
8. ☐ Confirm the review rule: ask happy customers in person, point them to the store's Google review link; never offer anything in exchange, never post as a customer.
9. ☐ One page summary handed to each salesperson: encouraged, prohibited, disclosure, who to ask. The summary is a store document approved by Shannon before it is distributed.
10. ☐ Log the session date, attendee count, and questions raised in the store's training record (internal; part of `TRAIN_THE_TRAINER_PLAYBOOK.md` training log). Nothing about individual salespeople is recorded.

## 9. Adapting per store

The GM adopts, edits, or declines this framework. Suggested per-store adaptation points:

1. **Store 01 and Store 02 (Kia, FL).** Same OEM, different markets. Pricing-practice emphasis can differ: high-turnover metros need the "check the listing first" rule stressed.
2. **Store 03 and Store 04 (Kia, TN, one GM).** The GM may adopt one version for both rooftops. Separate records, separate team sessions per rooftop — the teams are different people even if the GM is the same.
3. **Store 05 (Ford, MO).** Different OEM and a different buyer base (skews older, Facebook-heavy per `reporting/GM_KPI_FRAMEWORK.md` §2.5). The disclosure emphasis and the "customers are watching on Facebook" angle land differently here; the GM shapes the examples.
4. **Any store may add rules** (e.g., no posting during customer appointments, no phones on the lot) — additions are the GM's call and go through Shannon before the store's version is distributed.
