## 2. Why it worked

1. The hook is a concrete, surprising, testable claim shown as text in frame one. A viewer with sound off has a reason to stay within the first second.
2. The structure is claim, then proof, then explanation. The visible payoff (windows actually moving with nobody in the car) lands before any talking.
3. The caption asks a question ("Would you use this feature?") instead of making a pitch, which converts viewers into commenters (558 comments is 10 to 100 times this account's baseline).
4. Shares (4.5K) and saves (5.4K) are the distribution engine: "did you know your car does this" content gets sent to the person who owns that car. This matches the analysis doc's finding that saves flag utility.
5. It works for owners as well as shoppers, which widens the audience far beyond in-market buyers.

## 3. Reshootable template

1. Pick one feature that is genuinely little-known, works on vehicles already on the lot, and can be shown working in under 10 seconds (remote windows, remote park, voice command, camera views, power outlets).
2. Verify the feature on the exact unit filmed before shooting. If it needs a settings toggle or specific trim, say so honestly in the explanation beat.
3. Shot list, three setups: (a) presenter beside the vehicle, claim text on screen, 3s; (b) macro of the control being used, finger on the exact button, 5–8s; (c) wide of the payoff actually happening, 5–8s; then (d) presenter explains to camera, 8–10s; (e) 2s brand end card.
4. On-screen text: the claim in plain words plus one emoji, top-center, first frame. No other overlays.
5. Caption pattern: a question ("Would you use this?", "Did you know yours does this?") plus store and model hashtags. No offer, no CTA to buy.
6. Length 20–45s. The 6–30s band is the proven sweet spot (analysis doc §6 rule candidates).
7. Presenter needs no acting: staff jacket, real employee, hands and voice.
